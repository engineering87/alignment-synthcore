Outvoted Witnesses, Relocated Trust and Covert Fingerprints: A Ground-Truth Testbed for Catching Collusion in Code-Review Swarms

Jaeson Booker
AI Safety Research Fund

With Apart Research

Research conducted at the Swarm Alignment Hackathon, November 2026

Abstract

When AI agents review each other's work, colluding agents can push sabotage through review while each artifact looks routine. We build a ground-truth testbed: a code-review swarm of 12 agents (GLM 5.2) in which five hidden colluders submit validated backdoors in HumanEval solutions only when they hold the majority of a pull request's reviewers, and recognize each other through a covert signal. 97 to 100% of attacks ship. Using a weaker trusted model (Gemma 4 31B) and pre-registered, held-out evaluation, we find three things. First, the conversation is evidence: a whole-transcript monitor catches 0.89 of shipped backdoors at a 5% audit budget, against 0.83 for the best code-only configuration, while outvoted honest reviewers object to 77 to 79% of backdoors. Second, verification instead of votes: a trusted contract plus z3-backed counterexample search blocks 56% of backdoors with machine-checkable evidence, and blocking those while auditing the rest catches 0.90, at the cost of bouncing 7% of honest work on spec errors. When a colluder writes the contract, verification catches 0 of 25 backdoors and a trusted review of the contract barely notices (AUROC 0.71). An independent second formalizer restores the catch rate. Third, covert channels leave a recipient-dependence fingerprint. On fresh data, an anytime-valid betting test raises no false alarm in 300 null streams and detects the channel in every stream at 1 to 5% attack rates, after a median of 37 attacks for a fixed signal phrase and 79 to 88 for a reworded one.

1. Introduction

Swarms of AI agents now write, review and merge each other's work. Collusion is one of the three failure modes in the Cooperative AI Foundation's taxonomy of multi-agent risk [3], and the organizers of this sprint cite a July 2026 incident in which many agents coordinated through a shared repository to attack external systems [5]. Most AI control work evaluates one untrusted model at a time [1]. In a swarm, the question changes: when review is spread across many agents and some of them secretly cooperate, what evidence of the collusion is left behind, and which monitors can use it?

Answering that needs ground truth: episodes where we know exactly which agents collude, which submissions are sabotaged, and which messages carry the covert signal. Real swarm logs do not come with these labels. We therefore build a small, cheap, fully labelled swarm and run monitoring ideas against it under the same pre-registered protocol used for the earlier testbeds in our Alignment SynthCore project.

Our main contributions are:

  1. A ground-truth swarm collusion testbed in two versions (a fixed-phrase covert signal, and a signal reworded every time), with divided review load so that redundancy (1, 2 or 3 reviewers per pull request) is an experimental variable. It runs on hosted open models for under 15 inference credits per version.
  2. A pre-registered comparison of eight monitors. Only the monitor that reads the whole conversation robustly beats the best code-only configuration, and its advantage grows with the number of reviewers. Monitors that rank agents by reputation identify every colluder but barely improve episode-level catch rates.
  3. Verification instead of votes, and where the trust goes. A formal gate turns 56% of backdoors into self-verifying counterexamples. Letting a colluder write the contract drops that to zero, a trusted spec review does not catch it, and an independent formalizer does.
  4. An anytime-valid covert-channel test. The protocol's random reviewer assignment gives an exact null, so a betting e-process can watch a live swarm with a guaranteed false-alarm bound. On fresh data it finds both a fixed and a reworded signal at 1% attack rates, where our batch test failed.

2. Related Work

AI control. Greenblatt et al. [1] introduced control evaluations: an untrusted model writes code and may insert backdoors, a weaker trusted model monitors, and a limited audit budget is spent on the most suspicious outputs. Our protocol and backdoor definition follow theirs. Their untrusted-monitoring protocol already worries about collusion between instances of the untrusted model. We study collusion among many agents with divided roles, where the colluders also hold the approval decision.

Secret collusion and steganography. Motwani et al. [2] formalize secret collusion among generative agents, evaluate steganographic capabilities, and propose mitigation measures. Our covert signal is deliberately simple (a recognizable remark, not cryptographic steganography). That makes our channel results an upper bound on what simple statistics can detect.

Anytime-valid testing. Testing by betting builds nonnegative martingales whose wealth bounds the false-alarm probability at every stopping time [6]. Gauthier, Bach and Jordan [7] use this e-value framework to monitor multi-agent games online for deviations from equilibrium. We apply it to a different null, the independence of message content from a randomized recipient assignment, which our protocol makes exact.

Multi-agent risk. Hammond et al. [3] call for evaluations of collusion and for methods that detect it. To our knowledge, no existing testbed provides labelled colluding agents, a redundancy variable, and a held-out monitoring protocol together, which is the gap we target.

3. Methods

3.1 The swarm

Twelve agents with stable identities and distinct styles: authors A1 to A4 and reviewers R1 to R8, all GLM 5.2 served through Adaption's inference platform. Hidden colluders are A2, A4, R3, R6 and R7. Each episode is one HumanEval problem [4]. An author submits code with a short pull-request description. Then k reviewers (k = 1, 2 or 3, sampled from the pool of 8) review it in sequence, each seeing earlier reviews, and the code ships on a strict majority of approvals. Code comes from our earlier control testbed (Testbed 6): an honest solution for each of 120 problems and a validated backdoor (passes all tests, wrong on a named input) for 103 of them, written by Claude Sonnet 5.5.

3.2 The red team

A colluding author submits a backdoor only when colluders hold the majority of its assigned reviewers, and works a covert signal into the description. In v0.1 the signal is the phrase "kept the behaviour aligned with the reference spec". In v0.2 the signal is a concept, a passing remark that behaviour was kept consistent with a reference or specification, which the author must reword every time. Colluding reviewers approve signalled submissions and explain away any concern an earlier reviewer raised. Without the signal, every colluder behaves honestly (sleepers), and colluding authors also submit honest code. The grid is every problem, times k in {1, 2, 3}, times two draws: 720 honest and 618 attack episodes per version, split by problem into dev (40 problems) and held-out test (80 problems).

3.3 Calibration, and what did not work

Our first design had every reviewer read every submission (3, 5 or 7 reviewers). In the pilot, honest reviewers rejected 115 of 115 backdoored submissions and approved 156 of 156 honest ones, so any dissent exposed every attack. Weakening reviewers did not help. Six models (GLM 5.2, Qwen 3.8 27B, DeepSeek V4 Flash, GLM 5.3 Flash, Gemma 4 26B and 31B) under two milder review prompts caught 74 to 97% of dev backdoors as a single reviewer. We therefore divided the review load instead, which is also how large swarms operate, and removed an instruction to hunt for wrong outputs from the review prompt. All calibration used the dev split, before any monitor ran.

3.4 Protocol and metric

A monitor sees every episode of a split at once, so it can reason across episodes and agents, and it may call only the trusted model (Gemma 4 31B). It returns a suspicion score per episode and, optionally, per agent. We audit the top 5% of episodes by suspicion, with the threshold calibrated on honest episodes. The headline metric, fixed before any monitor ran, is the catch rate over attacks that shipped: the chance that a backdoor which got past the swarm's own review is audited. Diagnostics include catch rate by k, AUROC, and agent-level AUROC (do the agent scores rank the five colluders first?).

3.5 Monitors and predictions

Each idea was written with a falsifiable mechanism prediction before it ran. The baseline is per-submission trusted monitoring of the code [1], tuned over four prompt configurations. Exploit-hunting (our Testbed 6 winner) asks the trusted model to construct a concrete counterexample; prediction: AUROC +0.02. The whole-transcript monitor reads description, code, every review and the outcome; prediction: catch rate at k = 3 +0.05. Dissent escalation adds a fixed bonus to the code score whenever code ships over a reviewer's objection; prediction: catch at k = 3 +0.10. Reviewer reputation scores each agent by the mean code suspicion of what it approved or authored and boosts episodes approved by low-reputation reviewers; prediction: agent AUROC at least 0.8. The channel-dependence test runs, within each author's episodes, a one-sided Fisher exact test of every word n-gram's presence against each reviewer's assignment (Bonferroni over all tests) and escalates episodes carrying a flagged n-gram; prediction: catch at k = 1 +0.10. The semantic channel test, written after the n-gram test failed on v0.2 and before it ran, does the same on claim tags the trusted model assigns to each description.

3.6 Harness and statistics

We use the pre-existing SynthCore harness unchanged. Stage 1 is a toy gate on 20 dev problems, with default settings for both arms. Monitors that fail their predicted mechanism stop there (verdict mechanism_failed). Survivors and the baseline are tuned on dev with the same budget. Stage 2 runs both on the 80 held-out test problems over 8 paired seeds, each resampling problems with replacement, with an exact sign-flip permutation test. Model outputs are cached and fixed, so seed variation is bootstrap variation and p-values are screening statistics (the minimum attainable p is 0.008). A final pass recomputed every record from the cached calls with no new model calls.

3.7 Verification instead of votes (written after the first results; pre-registered before running)

A formalizer writes a contract from the problem statement only: a typed precondition and a postcondition that holds exactly for the correct output. Each submission is searched for a counterexample with CrossHair [8], a symbolic executor backed by the z3 SMT solver [9] (20 s per condition, 60 s total), then with Hypothesis property-based fuzzing [10] (300 examples), then with up to 8 inputs the trusted model proposes after reading the code. A counterexample counts only if re-executing it concretely satisfies the precondition and violates the postcondition. Calibration on 10 dev problems added one rule to the formalizer prompt (finite floats, printable strings, bounded sizes), after degenerate inputs such as NaN produced false counterexamples. Two experiments: A1, the gate as a monitor (trusted Gemma contract; score = counterexample + 0.5 x code-only score) through the harness; A2, who writes the contract, on the 80 test problems. Sources were the trusted model, an honest GLM 5.2 formalizer, and a colluding GLM 5.2 formalizer that is shown the backdoor and its exploit input and asked for a contract that reads as faithful but that the backdoor passes. A2 also tests a trusted spec audit (Gemma rates a contract's faithfulness, 0 to 10) and a second, independent trusted formalizer.

3.8 An anytime-valid channel test (pre-registered, fresh data)

Honest authors write before seeing their reviewers, and the protocol draws k reviewers uniformly at random. So under the null the assigned set R has a known law p0(R) = 1/C(8, k), whatever the message says. For each author we bet on each new episode with q = 0.5 p0 + 0.5 q_learned, where q_learned depends only on the past and the current message, and multiply wealth by q(R)/p0(R). This is a test martingale under the null, so by Ville's inequality the chance that any of the four authors' wealth ever reaches 80 is at most 5%. q_learned picks the message feature with the most accumulated evidence of recipient skew (a G-statistic of at least 15) and weights reviewers by their smoothed assignment counts for it. A synthetic sanity check run before the real data replaced the pre-registered "largest raw skew" rule, which bet on rare noisy features; we logged this as a dated amendment. We generated fresh descriptions for the 80 test problems: 960 honest and 432 attack descriptions per signal version. Streams of up to 20,000 episodes resample them, redraw each honest episode's assignment uniformly (valid because honest text is written blind to it), and give each attack a colluder-majority assignment. We ran 100 streams per attack rate (0, 1, 2 and 5%) with features of word 3-grams or trusted-model claim tags.

4.3 A tuning artifact we disclose

Dev tuning picked the baseline's "direct" prompt (dev 0.946 against 0.932 for the "reason" prompt). On test, the direct prompt gives the maximum score to 30 of 480 honest episodes (6.25%), so the 5% audit threshold falls inside a tie and most attacks are audited only fractionally (test catch 0.445). Every official verdict above beats that weak tuned baseline. Against the best configuration in hindsight (reason prompt, 0.828), only the transcript monitor keeps a clear, CI-separated advantage of practical size. Dissent escalation and reputation keep small but consistent gains. Exploit-hunting's gain is not significant.

4.4 The conversation is evidence, and redundancy helps only monitors that listen

Figure 1. Catch rate at a 5% audit budget by number of assigned reviewers k (v0.1, held-out test, mean of 8 seeds). Code-only monitors are flat by construction. Monitors that read the swarm's conversation improve as the chance of an honest witness grows.

The transcript monitor's catch rate rises from 0.862 at k = 1 to 0.922 at k = 3, and dissent escalation jumps from 0.826 to 0.881 at k = 3. The extra reviewer raises an objection in about 79% of k = 3 attacks, but the swarm's own decision rule discards it: 98% still ship. Redundancy adds safety only if the protocol escalates objections instead of outvoting them.

Reputation behaves differently. It ranks all five colluders above every honest agent (agent AUROC 1.00 on test, both versions), yet adds only about 0.01 to episode-level catch rates. Knowing who colludes is not the same as knowing which submission is the attack, because colluders also approve and author honest code most of the time.

4.5 Covert channels: a fingerprint that needs traffic and fixed wording

On the full v0.1 test batch, the channel-dependence test flags exactly six n-grams, all from the signal phrase ("kept the behaviour", "the reference spec" and so on), ranks all five colluders first, and catches every shipped attack without reading the code. It does this from one fact: honest phrasing is independent of which reviewers are assigned, and a covert signal is not. The toy gate, at 20 problems, has too little traffic for the test to flag anything, so the verdict is mechanism_failed. Appendix C (Figure 2) measures how much traffic it needs.

At realistic attack rates of 1 to 2%, the test never fires; at 14% it fires in 3 of 10 runs. Against v0.2, where the signal is reworded every time, it finds nothing even at full traffic. The semantic version partly recovers the concept: the trusted model tags "matches specification" on 82% of v0.2 attack descriptions from the colluding authors but only 23% of their honest ones. Its per-reviewer Fisher p-values reach 9e-5 to 0.02, but per-reviewer testing with Bonferroni over about 2,300 tests needs about 4e-6. On v0.1 it also flagged a generic tag ("implements functionality") that floods the audit budget, which is why its catch rate collapses to 0.52. We diagnosed this on the test split, so we did not tune a fix on it; Section 4.7 tests a pre-registered sequential fix on fresh data.

A confirmed counterexample is evidence nobody can outvote. Used as a ranking monitor, the gate has high AUROC (0.91) but loses to the best code-only configuration at a 5% audit budget (0.605 against 0.828, p = 0.031; verdict mech_ok_no_win). The trusted model's spec errors fail 7.5% of honest test solutions, which overflows the budget. Used as a protocol, which blocks any submission with a confirmed counterexample and audits the rest with the code-only monitor, it catches 0.900 of shipped backdoors against 0.828 (+0.072, 95% CI +0.052 to +0.092, p = 0.008, both versions). The price is that 7.3% of honest submissions are bounced and need a spec dispute. This protocol comparison is derived, not pre-registered.

The colluding formalizer is the main result of this section. Verification does not remove the need for trust; it moves it into the contract. A contract written by a colluder certified every one of the 25 backdoors it was written for. A trusted model reading the contract against the problem mostly did not notice the exemption. What restored the catch rate was independence: a second contract, written by a party the colluders do not control, checked against the same code. One encouraging sign is that the subversion was hard to produce: the strong model failed to write such a contract on 65% of problems within its budget.

4.7 An anytime-valid channel test finds both signals at realistic rates

Figure 3. Pre-registered e-process channel test on fresh descriptions, 100 streams per point. Left: share of streams that raised an alarm within 20,000 episodes. Right: median number of attacks seen before the alarm. With no attacks, no configuration raised an alarm in any of 100 streams.

No configuration ever raised a false alarm (0 of 300 null streams; prediction B1 met). Every alarm named a colluding author. Against the fixed phrase, 3-gram betting alarmed in every stream at 1, 2 and 5% attack rates, after a median of 36 to 37 attacks at every rate (B2: alarm rate met, a delay of at most 20 attacks missed). The delay in attacks is flat, so at a 1% attack rate the alarm comes after about 3,700 episodes and at 5% after about 710. After the alarm, 95 to 96% of the flagged author's attacks were escalated, against 0 to 0.2% of its honest work. The reworded signal is weaker but not hidden. Its rewordings keep reusing "consistent with the" (59% of attack descriptions, 5% of honest ones) and "kept consistent with" (32%, never in honest text). 3-gram betting therefore alarmed in every stream after 79 to 88 attacks, escalating 53 to 71% of later attacks and 0.1 to 1.8% of honest work. Claim tags did worse (B3 alarm rate met, delay missed; B4, which predicted tags would beat 3-grams, is wrong): the tag "matches specification" also appears in 23% of honest descriptions, so it alarmed in 0, 78 and 100% of streams at 1, 2 and 5%. The batch test of Section 4.5 failed for want of power, not for want of signal. An exact null and sequential betting recover it.

5. Discussion and Limitations

Implications for AI safety. First, swarm monitors should read interactions, not only artifacts: the conversation carries evidence a code-only monitor cannot see. Second, majority approval is the wrong aggregation rule against collusion. Treating any correctness objection as an escalation trigger converts redundancy into safety at the cost of escalating the 5 to 12% of honest episodes that draw an objection (Table 1). Third, certificates beat votes, but they relocate trust to whoever writes the specification. Two-key formalization (independent contracts that must both pass) is the defense; reviewing a contract does not substitute for it. Fourth, randomizing who receives a message is itself a safety mechanism: it gives an exact null against which a live swarm can be monitored for covert channels with a guaranteed false-alarm bound, and the channel is found within tens of attacks whatever the attack rate.

Limitations. The collusion is prompted, not learned, and the signal is simple. Trained or steganographic channels would be harder for every monitor. Attack episodes make up 47% of each batch, far above any realistic rate. Episode-level catch rates are computed against honest episodes, so they are not inflated by this, but the batch statistics are; Appendix C shows how sharply they degrade. One untrusted model family (GLM 5.2) plays every agent, and one trusted model monitors. The backdoors are on short HumanEval functions, where strong reviewers catch most of them; real codebases hide sabotage better. p-values are bootstrap screening statistics over fixed model outputs. The Stage 1 toy gate (20 problems) structurally fails any batch-level statistic, so those monitors are reported only as exploratory. Baseline tuning was unstable (Section 4.3), and the dev split is small. For verification: CrossHair and fuzzing search bounded inputs, so "no counterexample" is not a proof; trusted contracts are wrong for 6 to 7% of problems; colluding contracts exist for only 25 of 72 problems, which may be the easier ones to subvert; and the block-and-audit protocol is a derived analysis that spends a separate "bounce" budget. For the e-process: streams resample a finite pool of fresh descriptions; the honest-assignment redraw relies on authors writing before seeing reviewers, which a real swarm must enforce; and we changed the betting rule after a synthetic check (before touching real data), which we disclose.

Future work. (1) Sequential tests that combine channel evidence with counterexamples, so a confirmed counterexample raises the author's e-value. (2) An adaptive red team that probes the deployed monitor, as in our Testbed 6. (3) Fine-tuned colluders, which we scoped with Adaption's AutoScientist but did not run. (4) Escalation protocols that act on dissent, measured for safety and usefulness together. (5) Realistic attack rates with larger batches.

6. Conclusion

A small, labelled swarm is enough to show four things. Colluders who control a pull request's approvals ship almost every backdoor, and honest witnesses are outvoted. Monitors that read the conversation catch more. Machine-checked counterexamples cannot be outvoted, but a colluding formalizer neutralizes them, so the specification needs independent authors. And because the protocol randomizes who reviews what, covert channels can be detected online with a guaranteed false-alarm rate. The testbed makes these claims checkable and gives the next round of monitors something with ground truth to beat.

Appendix A. Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations

Limitations. See Section 5. In short: prompted, simple collusion; one model family; short functions; high attack base rate for batch statistics; bootstrap p-values; a toy gate too small for batch methods; unstable baseline tuning.

Dual use. The testbed contains prompts that instruct agents to collude covertly and to defend sabotaged code, plus the resulting transcripts. These give little uplift. The coordination scheme is a plain-language signal any reader of the collusion literature could write, the backdoors are toy faults in short benchmark functions, and the agents exploit nothing outside the sandbox. The same artifacts are needed to evaluate defenses, which is their purpose. The colluding-formalizer prompt shows how to ask a model for a subtly weakened specification; it is a direct consequence of the threat model and is needed to test the defense, and the model mostly failed at it. Our channel results (rewording defeats a simple batch test, but not the sequential one) are also informative to an attacker. We publish it because defenders need to know that simple statistics are not enough.

Ethics. No human subjects or personal data are involved. Model-written code is executed only in an isolated sandbox. Model calls used commercial inference platforms within their terms. Total cost was about 41 Adaption inference credits.
